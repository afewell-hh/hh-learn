Neocloud operations

The hard part happens before anyone types a command

A customer signs for eight HGX B300 nodes. The eight you have free are not in the same rack. Say they hang off six different leaf switches — the exact number is yours to substitute, and the only part that matters is that it is more than one and you did not get to choose it.

Until those switches agree on exactly what this tenant is, the nodes earn nothing. On a hand-run EVPN/VXLAN fabric, getting them to agree is an engineering exercise, not a typing exercise.

What this story follows

One network, for one tenant. A GPU tenant usually needs several: a frontend network, two backend planes so compute traffic stays on its own rails, and something for management. Each is its own network, and every port on every node has to join the right one.

Putting all of that on one page would bury the point. So this follows the smallest unit of the job — one subnet reaching one node — and then multiplies it back up at the end. Most of what follows repeats for every network; a few of the choices are made once per tenant, and the arithmetic at the end keeps those apart.

Nine configuration choices to coordinate across leaves

For one of the tenant's networks. None of these are typed quickly and forgotten — each is a decision that has to hold across every switch that network touches, and stay true the next time the tenant grows. This is the configuration surface described in any vendor's EVPN/VXLAN design guide — Cisco's, for example.

  • The L2VNI for the subnetA network identifier that must be unique across the entire fabric, not just this switch.Reuse one another tenant already has, and two tenants quietly share a broadcast domain.
  • The L3VNI for the tenant's routing tableA second identifier, also fabric-unique, that carries routed traffic between leaves.Collide it, and traffic crosses between tenants that were meant to be separate.
  • Route targets for import and exportWhich routing tables may exchange routes with which.A single wrong digit leaks one tenant's routes into another, or cuts the tenant off from itself.
  • Symmetric or asymmetric IRB, and the transit network it needsHow routed traffic crosses the overlay, plus the extra VLAN and VNI that symmetric mode requires.Mix the two modes across leaves and routed traffic between them does not work.
  • The anycast gateway address and MACEvery leaf carrying the subnet must present the same gateway to the servers on it.Differ on one leaf and servers there see a different gateway from the rest of the subnet.
  • ARP and neighbour suppressionWhether each leaf answers address lookups locally instead of flooding them.On for some leaves and off for others, and behaviour changes with load.
  • The VLAN-to-VNI mapping on each leafLocal VLAN numbers may differ from switch to switch. The VNI they map to may not.Get one mapping wrong and that leaf's servers are in a different network from the rest of the tenant.
  • A BGP identity for the tenant's routing tableEach tenant VRF needs its own routing-protocol identity on every leaf that carries it.Allocate it to collide with something already deployed and the two interfere.
  • MTU, end to endOverlay encapsulation adds bytes. Every hop has to have room for them.Miss it on one hop and large frames are dropped there while small ones pass.

Here is why this is a security problem and not just an operations problem.

None of these failures raise an alarm. A reused VNI does not error — it works, and two tenants are in the same broadcast domain. A mistyped route target does not error — it advertises routes somewhere they should never go. The fabric stays up, the dashboards stay green, and the thing you have built is not the thing you designed.

The blast radius of one wrong value is not one switch. It is the separation between two customers.

This is the most common way networks break

The industry has been measuring this for years.

“Network-related issues continue to account for the largest share of IT service outages… As in previous years, these are driven primarily by configuration and change management failures.”

Uptime Institute, Annual Outage Analysis 2026, Figure 11 (n=122). Configuration and change management has been the leading cause of network-related outages in every Uptime resiliency survey since 2021.
92% of operators say human error was at least a minor contributor to their most recent significant outage. Uptime Institute, Annual Outage Analysis 2026, Fig. 13, n=220. Uptime treats human error as a contributing factor, not a root cause.
87% of those who had an impactful outage say it could have been avoided with better management, processes or configuration. Uptime Institute, Annual Outage Analysis 2026, citing its 2025 data centre survey, n=98. Up seven points on the prior year.
~70% of outages come from changes to a live system — not from things breaking on their own. Google, Site Reliability Engineering (O'Reilly, 2016). Google's own operational experience, stated without a published methodology — read it as testimony, not as research.
1 in 5 say their most recent major outage cost them more than $1 million. For 57%, more than $100,000. Uptime Institute, Annual Outage Analysis 2026, n=94. Uptime deliberately publishes no average cost, because outlier events distort it.

And Uptime is explicit about why the number is moving. Their 2025 analysis says the rise in IT and networking outages is “likely caused by increased IT and network complexity, leading to issues with change management and misconfigurations.”

The failure mode here is not incompetence. It is competent people operating something with more moving parts than any one of them can hold in their head — which is a fair description of a multi-plane, rail-optimised AI fabric running EVPN/VXLAN.

Abridged for reading: the DHCP range and namespaces are omitted. The full file is 27 lines. A whole tenant is several of these — one network declaration each, and one attachment per port per node — all of the same shape.

The declaration supplies four things: a subnet, a gateway address, a VLAN number, and which port the server is on. Search it for vni, route-target, irb, suppress, mtu, bgp, evpn or vxlan and you will not find any of them.

Read the amber lines as the job description. Every one of those lines is something a person allocates, records somewhere the next person will find it, applies to six switches, and verifies — then locates again the next time this tenant changes. Wherever that record lives, the fabric cannot read it — so nothing but the person checks whether this allocation collides with an existing one.

On the right, none of those values was typed per leaf: the controller derived them from the declaration. What this capture shows is the configuration that resulted on the one leaf the server attached to. It does not measure what landed on any other switch — see the boundary note at the end.

One hundred and twenty applications of nine choices — and the fabric does not itself reconcile them against a record of what you already allocated. You find out from a tenant who cannot reach their own storage, or from a security review that discovers two customers in the same broadcast domain.

The Hedgehog side of the same tenant is four network declarations and one attachment per port. The operator still chooses subnets, gateways and VLAN numbers; what they do not do is allocate and track the identifiers behind them. Adding the ninth node is the same declaration again, not a new design exercise.

Eight HGX nodes is 64 GPUs. Priced at Lambda's published list rate for B200 capacity at scale — $8.87 per GPU per hour — that is the rentable value of the capacity standing idle while the switches are brought into agreement:

$568per hour $13,600per day $95,000per week

The customer has signed. The nodes are racked and powered. The only thing between them and earning is a set of switches agreeing on what this tenant is.

Read this as the value of the asset while it waits, not as a bill. It assumes the capacity would otherwise be earning at a published list rate, which is the premise of the scenario rather than a measurement of anyone's contract.

Rate from lambda.ai/pricing, read 10 November 2026: HGX B200, 256+ GPUs, 2 weeks to 1 year — their lowest published tier, so this is the conservative end. B300 has no public list price; B200 is the nearest published capacity. 64 × $8.87 = $567.68/hr.

And capacity you own still strands

The largest production GPU fleet ever characterised in public found that owning the GPUs is not the same as being able to allocate them — and named the network as one of the reasons.

“High GPU demand does not yield high effective utilization: idle GPUs frequently become unallocatable because free capacity is stranded across nodes, lacks matching CPUs, or violates network-locality constraints.”

Alibaba Serverless Infrastructure, characterised over six months across 155,410 GPUs and 14 million jobs. USENIX OSDI 2026. The same paper defines GPU allocation as “the fraction of total GPU capacity claimed by jobs — which reflects how effectively resources are claimed and directly affects revenue.”

Their Figure 13 measures how much idle capacity cannot be handed out, by the shape of the request. For a single GPU it is 5%. For an eight-GPU request with 128 CPUs beside it, it is 28% — larger, more constrained placements strand more than small ones. The paper attributes that unallocatable capacity to GPUs stranded across nodes and to insufficient CPUs beside them — explicitly not to fractional-GPU fragmentation, which it finds negligible. Network locality appears in its own summary of why idle GPUs become unallocatable; the figure does not break out how much of the 28% it accounts for, and neither will we.

This is also why it compounds. In the hand-run model the next tenant is the whole exercise again, on a fabric that now has more existing allocations to collide with. The work does not get easier as the cluster fills. It gets harder, and one inconsistency costs more.

What we actually measured

One tenant, applied to a live fabric

A Hedgehog VLAB — one spine, two SONiC switches, two servers — on hhfab v0.51.4 and SONiC 4.5.2. We read every switch's running configuration, applied one VPC and one VPCAttachment, waited for the fabric to converge, and read it again.

On the one leaf the tenant's server attached to, 35 configuration objects and 24 lines of routing configuration appeared, from 27 lines of declared intent. Every value in the table above comes from that difference.

That is the slice: one network, one node, one switch. The 120 figure above is that slice multiplied by the tenant in the opening — we did not build the eight-node tenant.

What this does not show

  • The lab is three switches and one network. The four-network, six-switch tenant is the shape of a real deployment, not something we ran.
  • This is configuration — control-plane evidence. It shows what each switch was told to be, not measured traffic behaviour between tenants.
  • The nine choices are the configuration surface a hand-run EVPN/VXLAN deployment has to coordinate. We did not build one alongside to race it.
  • Route distinguishers and broadcast replication are fabric-wide choices made once at build time, so they did not appear in a per-tenant difference.
